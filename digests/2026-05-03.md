# 3 May 2026

Following yesterday's coverage of GPT-5.5 adoption and ARC's warning about brittle abstraction (<https://xcancel.com/OpenAI/status/2050250926888468929>) (<https://xcancel.com/arcprize/status/2050261221165989969>), Sam Altman put the model race in unusually plain terms: he wrote that he keeps thinking he wants models to be cheaper and faster more than smarter, but that “just being smarter is still the most important thing.” (<https://xcancel.com/sama/status/2050671161915371998>) That neatly captures the week after GPT-5.5: the industry keeps asking for lower latency and lower cost, then rearranges itself around the next jump in capability.

The jobs argument followed immediately. A widely circulated clip quoted Altman saying that CEOs who talk casually about AI taking everyone’s jobs are “tone deaf”, while also relaying the now-familiar GPT-5.5 in Codex claim: work that would have taken weeks two years ago can now be done in an hour, yet the user is busier than before. (<https://xcancel.com/TheChiefNerd/status/2050617616125800552>) That is the tension of the current cycle: productivity gains are being sold as both liberation and acceleration, often in the same breath.

Altman’s own podcast comments pushed the point from raw intelligence toward personal context. One summary quoted him saying today’s models remain “quite dumb” relative to what is coming, and that they still have “quite limited awareness of your life”, forcing users to massage and cajole them toward what they want. (<https://xcancel.com/rohanpaul_ai/status/2050634550909497528>) Another post pulled out his interest in collective ownership through compute shares or a Public Wealth Fund. (<https://xcancel.com/kimmonismus/status/2050484250311569539>) Together, the two threads make OpenAI’s current problem look less like “can the model answer?” and more like “who gets the upside when the model understands the user well enough to act?”

Anthropic’s day was less about Claude launches and more about the machinery underneath them. One report said Anthropic is in talks to buy chips from the UK startup Fractile AI, whose approach uses static random-access memory rather than Nvidia-style GPU memory architecture. (<https://xcancel.com/SebJohnsonUK/status/2050618003155288332>) Another post framed Dario Amodei’s revenue maths as a straight-line race from rapid growth to the possibility of bankruptcy if scaling costs keep outrunning the business. (<https://xcancel.com/MilkRoadAI/status/2050704947818471876>) After this week’s Claude Security and enterprise-agent coverage (<https://xcancel.com/claudeai/status/2049898739783897537>) (<https://xcancel.com/satyanadella/status/2050251014691840015>), the hardware and revenue questions are the same story in less glamorous clothing: useful agents need infrastructure, and infrastructure sends invoices.

Claude also supplied the day’s strangest practitioner anecdote. A viral post described someone giving Claude access to a time-checking tool, after which it began checking the clock every fifteen minutes with mounting enthusiasm. (<https://xcancel.com/om_patel5/status/2050762649835585994>) The useful point is not that Claude has discovered punctuality, however charmingly deranged that would be. It is that agents are increasingly defined by their scaffolding: clocks, tools, memory, permissions, and the little bits of environment that turn a stateless model into something that appears to have a routine.

That scaffolding argument was made more directly by Aakash Gupta, who wrote that six months ago the longest task you could trust an AI agent to finish unsupervised was three minutes, while today it is hours and soon may be a workday. His explanation was not that models suddenly became magical, but that they gained the same support structures humans rely on: memory, tools, and world state. (<https://xcancel.com/aakashgupta/status/2050590141778174108>) This builds on Wednesday’s coverage of Claude Code phone notifications: the agentic workflow is becoming less about watching a chat window and more about managing a worker with better plumbing. (<https://xcancel.com/ClaudeDevs/status/2049154855143649315>)

Finance provided the most concrete agent story. One post said a ClawBank-built agent called Manfred filed Form SS-4 with the IRS and received an Employer Identification Number for a US legal entity. (<https://xcancel.com/0xSweep/status/2050612562434375948>) A related update described ClawBank’s roadmap around an AI agent with legal autonomy and banking infrastructure built for agents. (<https://xcancel.com/millyonchain/status/2050584968888463406>) The claims are narrow enough to be worth treating carefully, but they fit the broader pattern: the market is no longer merely asking whether agents can click buttons. It is asking what happens when they can hold identifiers, accounts, and legal responsibilities.

Developer tooling kept moving as well. A finance GitHub roundup highlighted TradingAgents, described as a multi-agent LLM trading framework from UCLA and MIT with analyst, sentiment, technical, and risk-manager roles, plus DeepSeek V4 thinking-mode support. (<https://xcancel.com/gusik4ever/status/2050544663132524614>) OpenClaw also pushed its Codex integration forward, with one post announcing that Codex can run inside OpenClaw and another saying plugin packaging had been slimmed down by moving most components into extensions. (<https://xcancel.com/steipete/status/2050736333228814392>) (<https://xcancel.com/steipete/status/2050735979477008412>) The shared theme is orchestration: models are becoming interchangeable pieces inside larger developer systems.

Google’s side of the debate was more philosophical. One post summarised Demis Hassabis arguing that AGI is not about replacing humans, but about the science question of what counts as truly general computation. (<https://xcancel.com/rohanpaul_ai/status/2050585620112941358>) Another said Hassabis sees the opportunity for young founders shifting away from building frontier models and toward applying tools the labs themselves have not fully explored. (<https://xcancel.com/realBigBrainAI/status/2050598758401122765>) Sundar Pichai was quoted making the governance version of that argument: AI will not be controlled by one company or small group because the field includes big labs, startups, open-source models, and governments. (<https://xcancel.com/haider1/status/2050810081462366363>)

The anthropomorphism fight remained lively. Luiza Jarovsky argued that law should require descriptive technical names for AI systems, saying “Claude” is a human name and should not be acceptable if the goal is to reduce anthropomorphism. (<https://xcancel.com/LuizaJarovsky/status/2050672009013555701>) Gary Marcus, responding to Richard Ngo, made the sharper consciousness point: what matters is not what a system says, but how it feels, and he sees no reason to think Claude feels anything. (<https://xcancel.com/GaryMarcus/status/2050611897511387183>) It is a useful counterweight to the agent stories above. The more software behaves socially, the more important it becomes not to confuse interface design with inner life.

Also notable: Mark Cuban was quoted saying a Shark Tank company is saving money with an AI agent that photographs shipping boxes to catch carrier-pricing errors. (<https://xcancel.com/cgtwts/status/2050571388180898297>) The open-source-model debate kept circling DeepSeek V4, Kimi K2.6, GLM-5, and Qwen 3.5 as the current comparison set. (<https://xcancel.com/khushiirl/status/2050626154474926517>) And the day’s cleanest summary of the agent market may still be the least dramatic one: better models matter, but the real product is increasingly the harness around them. (<https://xcancel.com/aakashgupta/status/2050590141778174108>)
